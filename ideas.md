# Ideas: markets where *winning* is the reward and *paying* is the revenue

> Thesis: sell a prize that costs nothing to produce, to people who are buying an identity rather than a product.
> The user's framing: **"winning is a reward for people and money for me."**

This is a living log. Each entry gets a description (what the business is) and a business model (how it makes money). New ideas are added continuously — see [§4](#4-the-idea-log) for the idea log, and [§9](#9-races-between-people-the-wider-theory) for the theory of **contests between individuals** (where the race itself is the revenue engine, rather than a shared identity).

## Index: every idea in one line

*One line each, so the whole map is visible before the detail. Every line says what the thing is, who pays, and — per the payer's-return test — whether it actually pays. **Pays** = the payer gets something measurable back. **Novelty** = the payer gets expression only, so it spikes and stops. **Needs audience** = it works, but only once a crowd exists.*

### A. Place identity — the "my city" engine
- **A1 Most beautiful city in the world** — $1 adds one to your city's tally, paid by hometown pride; **novelty** unless the contest is framed honestly as a popularity tally.
- **A2 Best small town in a country** — the same tally aimed at small towns, whose local newspapers give free coverage to any national placing; **needs audience**.
- **A3 Best neighbourhood in a metro** — narrower and cheaper, with rivalry that bites because neighbours actually know each other; **needs audience**.
- **A4 Best national dish / cuisine** — country-versus-country food pride, the biggest identity pool of all and the most flammable; **needs audience**.
- **A5 Ugliest roundabout / worst traffic / most beautiful pothole** — negative contests that are funnier, spread further, and give local media a reason to cover them; **novelty**.

### B. Group identity that already has money
- **B1 Best university** — alumni bid to lift their alma mater, and they already donate billions from pure identity, so you're relocating an existing spend; **pays**.
- **B2 Best high school in a state** — dense, lifelong school pride, but **minors must never be allowed to pay**; **needs audience**.
- **B3 Best fanbase in a league** — fans pay to rank their club's supporters above their rivals' — fandom is the purest ego engine that exists; **needs audience**.
- **B4 Best marching band / student section** — real subcultures with permanent rivalries and no existing ranking to fight on; **needs audience**.
- **B5 Best military unit / branch** — intense identity and sharing, but politically delicate; **needs audience**.

### C. Breed and tribe identity
- **C1 Cutest dog breed** — ~200 breeds, each with a ferociously loyal owner army that already argues about this for free; **needs audience**.
- **C2 Best rescue dog / cutest shelter dog** — the honest version of America's Favorite Pet, where the prize is an actual adoption and shelters promote it to their own donors; **pays**.
- **C3 Best cat breed / horse breed** — smaller pools than dogs but more money, especially horse people; **needs audience**.
- **C4 Best chicken / pigeon / rabbit breed** — tiny communities that are ferociously competitive and almost entirely unserved online; **needs audience**.

### D. Fandom identity — the fully validated lane
- **D1 Best K-pop group or idol** — already industrialised by voting apps with hundreds of thousands of users, so the opening is a seasonal bracket instead; **needs audience**.
- **D2 Best anime character** — fandoms already run multi-week vote campaigns unprompted, so you'd be monetising behaviour that exists; **needs audience**.
- **D3 Best video game character or NPC** — the same mechanic with an older audience that has money; **needs audience**.
- **D4 Best fictional ship** — shipping wars are the most durable argument on the internet and supply infinite seasons; **needs audience**.
- **D5 Best underrated band** — fans fund their favourite, and bands promote it themselves for free publicity; **needs audience**.

### E. Craft and profession identity
- **E1 Best tattoo artist in a city** — status-obsessed, Instagram-native and already competitive; **needs audience**.
- **E2 Best barista, barber or bartender in a city** — small and local, and these trades love a public ranking they can put in the window; **pays**.
- **E3 Best wedding vendor in a city** — vendors already spend $50–$1,200/month on placement and hate the sales-led incumbent, so a self-serve ranked badge displaces real budget; **pays**.
- **E4 Best indie game** — developers campaign their own audiences hard for a ranking; **needs audience**.

### F. Cause-wrapped identity — the strongest legitimacy layer
- **F1 Best rescue shelter in a state** — shelters rally their whole donor base, which is exactly the network you want; **pays**.
- **F2 Most deserving teacher** — parent groups and PTAs fund it, but **no payment from minors**; **pays**.
- **F3 Best local charity** — nonprofits bid their supporters in; **pays**.
- **F4 Best firehouse or ambulance crew** — beloved locally and reliably picked up by local press; **needs audience**.
- **F5 Best service dog program** — high emotional stakes with a genuine cause attached; **pays**.

### G. Absurd and comedy contests — cheapest to run, fastest to spread
- **G1 Worst haircut**, **G2 pettiest office grievance**, **G3 most cursed photo**, **G4 worst parked car in a city**, **G5 most beautiful pothole** — near-zero legitimacy risk, enormous shareability, and an honest silliness that makes paid voting feel less like a con; **needs audience** but is the cheapest first experiment here.

### H. Meta / self-aware
- **H1 Best pay-to-rank site** — a board ranking the boards, which earns free press from every competitor it lists; **novelty**.
- **H2 Vote on which idea I build next** — publish the log and charge $1 per vote, so the document itself becomes the launch and demand is tested before any code exists; **novelty**.

### I. Arena structure — how the clock runs
- **I1 Daily crown** — resets every 24 hours for maximum repeat payment and minimum sunk cost; **mechanic**.
- **I2 Monthly trophy plus Hall of Fame** — past champions stay listed forever, which is what makes winning permanent enough to be worth paying for; **mechanic**.
- **I3 Dynasty board** — an all-time board running alongside the seasonal one, giving two different motivations; **mechanic**.
- **I4 Decay / rent** — rank lapses unless renewed monthly, which is the fix for outbid.lol's one-time-purchase weakness; **mechanic**.

### J. Structural upgrades worth stealing
- **J1 Reciprocity hook** — let the payer pick the cause a share of their money goes to, turning vanity into donation with a leaderboard; **mechanic**.
- **J2 Proxy bidding** — let people back an entity they don't belong to, so the fan base isn't limited to locals; **mechanic**.
- **J3 Physical upsell** — sell the winner a framed certificate, trophy or plaque, which is high-margin and where emotional buyers convert hardest; **mechanic**.
- **J4 Badge embed** — hand winners an embeddable live badge, so every embed is a permanent free ad; **mechanic**.
- **J5 Free daily vote** — mandatory, because it builds the habit and keeps the board alive between paid bursts; **mechanic**.
- **J6 Public bid history** — showing who paid what and when *is* the content, which is what outbid.lol's whole draw was; **mechanic**.

### K. Races between people — individual competition
- **K1 Daily photo contest** — a specific daily theme plus paid "keys" and vote-skipping "fills"; proven at scale by GuruShots but **needs audience**.
- **K2 Head-to-head bracket battles** — public 1v1 elimination where the bracket is the content and manufactures "vote now, we're losing"; **needs audience**.
- **K3 Speed races with a third-party buyer** — timed coding or puzzle challenges where the underused revenue is **recruiters paying to see the leaderboard**; **pays**.
- **K4 Submission-fee awards** — the film-festival model: $50–$150 entry fees, a prize that costs nothing, and a rejection rate that proves the award means something; **pays**.
- **K5 Streak races** — 30-day consistency challenges where the drama is attrition and a subscription fits naturally; **needs audience**.
- **K6 Charity races** — walk-a-thons and team fundraisers where participants raise from their own networks, which is ancient and socially bulletproof; **pays**.
- **K7 Prediction races** — fantasy and prediction contests, deliberately last because they sit closest to **regulated gambling**; **do not start here**.
- **K8 Escalation / dare races** — who does the most ridiculous thing, viral by construction, with humour removing the legitimacy burden; **needs audience**.
- **K9 Curation-as-contest** — "best of the week" where the entrant is a business and the prize is a badge it displays; **pays**.

### L. Vanity tokens and personal-name boards
- **L1 Personal name bid board** — pay $1 more to hold the #1 name slot; **fails** — one payer, no rival, and no spectators to witness the status it sells.
- **L2 Surname, family or clan boards** — the salvage, because "the Smiths are #1" restores pooling, a named rival, and shareability; **needs audience** but is the right shape.
- **L3 Name and handle spend reality check** — domain money is real but it's **utility** spend for an address, not vanity spend for a mention, and the economics don't transfer; **warning**.

### M. "#1" concepts built for virality
- **M1 The Dethrone** — you pay to outbid a named holder and they're notified, so each outbid produces two posts and manufactures the rival a name board lacks; **pays** once, until decay is added.
- **M2 Turf war** — teams buy tiles that decay hourly, which fixes pooling, supplies the rivalry free, makes the map the shareable artifact, and turns the sale into rent; **the strongest candidate**.
- **M3 A board for every real rivalry** — iOS versus Android, tea versus coffee, tabs versus spaces, each with two pre-existing armies that will share it for you; **needs audience** but manufactures its own.
- **M4 Absurdly specific #1 boards** — "#1 at never having watched Star Wars", where the sharing motive is amusement rather than prestige, so the board never has to claim it matters; **needs audience**.
- **M5 Pay to say it** — the top slot holds a sentence the holder writes, so the content changes constantly and the current sentence is the ad; costs moderation and **is proven weak** at $365 by `oneword.global`.
- **M6 Closed auction with a countdown** — a midnight deadline turns the final minutes into clippable sniping drama, and a daily winner builds a Hall of Fame while the reset prevents staleness; **mechanic**.

### N. Paid rivalry voting — the iOS vs Android case
- **N1 The concept** — charge per vote in the permanent, 100%-coverage iOS-versus-Android war, sold as a costly signal rather than a poll; **the product is "put your money where your mouth is".**
- **N2 The $0.50 fee maths** — US card fees eat **63%** of a $0.50 vote while India's ~2% takes almost nothing, and bundles ("10 votes for $5") drop fees to ~9% and fix it in any market; **the pricing answer**.
- **N3 The QR verification idea** — a QR code is just a URL and user-agent checks are spoofable, so real device verification needs **App Attest / Play Integrity**; but a paid vote is its own anti-cheat, so rate-limit spend instead; **the technical answer**.
- **N4 Two upgrades** — the loser's pot funds the winner's chosen charity, and you run many rivalries rather than one; **mechanic**.

### O. Designed for Instagram — a built-in reason to post
- **O1 The Title Registry** — real-world titles auctioned publicly with a physical trophy mailed to the holder, sold largely as **gifts** so the recipient becomes the distributor, and defended monthly so revenue recurs; **the strongest fit**.
- **O2 The Challenge Chain** — paying is an act that names another person and notifies them, so growth is enforced by the mechanic instead of hoped for; **payment becomes distribution**.
- **O3 Event-timed national board** — the same backing board bound to a real event, where the deadline and the news cycle are the whole difference from `whos1.com`'s $205; **needs an event**.
- **O4 Live wall during a match** — fans pay to put colours or a message on a screen that updates during the game, time-boxed to 90 minutes so urgency is total; **needs an event**.
- **O5 Absurdist leaderboard** — comedy titles instead of prestige, because amusement is a far easier share than a boast; **needs audience**.

### P. Ideas where the payment justifies itself — the no-leap family *(full detail in the "Better ideas: where the payment justifies itself" section near the top)*
- **P1 Performance-priced recovery** — you rebuild failed or abandoned revenue for a niche and take ~20% of what you recover, so the buyer's downside is zero and the objection is structurally impossible; **pays, and needs no audience**.
- **P2 Mandatory compliance for one narrow niche** — the document, certificate or filing a trade legally must have, which lapses and therefore recurs; **pays**.
- **P3 The displayable credential** — a paid-for badge, score or verified metric whose criteria mean something, so it measurably wins work **and** markets itself; **pays — the best balance in the file**.
- **P4 Insurance-grade monitoring for a niche** — watching the one thing that costs real money when it breaks, cheap to run and bought out of fear; **pays**.
- **P5 The paperwork blocking a signed deal** — auto-answering vendor security questionnaires and client onboarding forms, justified trivially against the deal size; **pays**.
- **P6 Backup and export for a platform income depends on** — insurance for a livelihood on Etsy, Shopify or Substack; **pays**.
- **P7 No-show and revenue recovery for a local trade** — booking, reminders and waitlist fill for barbers, groomers and physios, where the recovered revenue is easy to count; **pays**.
- **P8 The annual filing chore for one country, one niche** — obligation, dread and recurrence, the three best motivators at once; **pays**.

### Q. Logged from our earlier conversation *(full detail in §7)*
- **Q1 outbid.lol** — rank equals cumulative dollars paid, which ratcheted to ~$200K+ gross but collapsed from ~1.09M visitors in 48 hours to ~6,453 a day in six weeks; **pays once**.
- **Q2 Early (Erly)** — a push-up alarm clock reaching ~$50K/month in four months, where the gimmick *was* the marketing; **pays** but only after months of distribution.
- **Q3 The wedding-vendor board** — the approved but paused pay-to-rank plan, now superseded by the tiered-badge design; **pays**.

**Dropped, and why:**

- **City / country pride boards** — already built by someone else and flat (`whos1.com` at $205, §10).
- **Generic pay-to-rank boards** — saturated and not monetizable as a category; 60+ clones listing on each other (§10).
- **Alarm-utility apps** — the model genuinely works, but it's a four-month distribution grind, not a fast path (§7.2).

- **Prediction / fantasy boards** — closest to regulated gambling; do not start there (§4 K7).

**The pattern in all five survivors:** the payer is buying **credibility or standing**, the buyer **displays** what they bought (so distribution is included), and the thing **decays** so it must be renewed. Stay inside that shape.

## The payer's return: the only question that matters

> The question, in the user's words: *"why will people pay? what's their profit? if whoever wins gets nothing, why would they spend a single penny?"*

This is the most important question in the file, and it's the one that kills most of the ideas logged here. So it gets its own section, before everything else.

### There usually *is* no financial profit — and that's fine

People routinely spend large amounts on things with zero financial return: football tickets, tattoos, flags, church donations, bumper stickers, pet costumes. The return isn't money. **The return is identity maintenance** — the purchase confirms and displays who you are, to yourself and to your in-group. Status is a genuinely valuable resource: it wins cooperation, trust, customers, mates, and better treatment. **So paying for status is not irrational.** It's the oldest purchase there is.

### But there is a hard condition, and it is the whole answer

**Status is only worth paying for when it is witnessed.** A costly signal with no audience is worth nothing — it's a message delivered to an empty room.

This is where the user's instinct is exactly right. A free click is worthless because **it costs nothing, so it proves nothing**. The entire function of money here is to be a **credible signal**: *"I paid for Android"* is a stronger claim than *"I voted for Android"* precisely because it cost something. **Paying isn't the barrier to the value — paying *is* the value.**

Which means the question "why would they pay?" has a precise answer:

> **They will pay for a message — but only if somebody is listening.**

The penny is the message. With no audience, there is no recipient, and no reason to send it.

### The three things that actually sustain payment

| What the payer gets | Is it real? | Works without an audience? |
|---|---|---|
| **Measurable return** — customers, leads, money, credibility that converts | Yes, verifiable | **Yes** |
| **Entertainment** — it is genuinely fun to play | Yes, psychic | **No** — needs a crowd to play against |
| **Witnessed status** — an audience watches you win | Yes, psychic | **No** — the audience *is* the product |

**Only the first one works without an audience.** That single line is the most useful practical conclusion in this entire file.

### The filter, applied to everything logged here

| Idea family | What the payer gets back | Sustains payment? |
|---|---|---|
| **Vendor badges** (G2 model, §11.5) | Customers and credibility — **measurable** | **Yes. Recurring. No audience needed.** |
| **Trade awards / submission fees** (§4 K4) | A laurel that demonstrably sells their work | **Yes**, annually, no audience needed |
| **Pay-to-prove** (§11.1) | Credibility that converts | **Yes**, no audience needed |
| **outbid.lol-style rank** | Traffic and status while it lasts | **Once** — no renewal, hence the collapse (§7.1) |
| **Pets / K-pop contests** | A prize plus fandom emotion | **Yes, but** requires existing scale and an audience |
| **GuruShots** | A game worth playing | Yes, but needs a crowd |
| **America's Favorite Pet** | A prize plus sunk cost | Yes — **predatory** (§3) |
| **City / pride boards** | Expression only | **No** — `whos1.com` at \$205 (§10) |
| **Personal name boards** | Expression only | **No** (§4 L1) |
| **iOS vs Android vote, as described** | Expression only — **no prize, no customers, no audience** | **No.** A one-time novelty spike |

### The uncomfortable conclusion

**Most ego schemes quietly depend on the payer not doing this arithmetic.** The honest question is the user's question: *what is the payer's return?* If you cannot answer it in one sentence, it isn't a business — it's a novelty, and it will spike once and stop (§7.1).

And that explains this whole log at once: **every idea that requires an audience loses to every idea that pays the payer back directly.** Which is exactly why the shortlist is led by vendor badges and awards — the two families where the payer's return is measurable on day one, with no crowd required.

## Better ideas: where the payment justifies itself

*The user's ask: ideas where you don't have to wonder "will they pay?" or "what do they get?" — where the payment is justified on its face.*

### The honest tension, stated first

**Justified payment and viral spread pull in opposite directions.** The thing that makes a payment obvious — *you need this* — is exactly what makes it unexciting to talk about. **Ego ideas spread and don't pay; necessities pay and don't spread.** Any plan that assumes both is fighting gravity.

The exception is the overlap: **products whose return is measurable *and* whose receipt is displayable.** That's the only place where the same artifact produces both revenue and distribution — and it's exactly why badges, certificates, verified scores and awards keep topping the shortlist.

### The six patterns that make payment self-evident

| Pattern | Why the payer doesn't hesitate | The test it passes |
|---|---|---|
| **Replacement** | It's the same thing they already buy — no new budget, no education | Is there *already* a line item for this? |
| **ROI arithmetic** | Pay X, provably get back >X | Can they compute the return in one napkin division? |
| **Legal necessity** | They cannot legally operate without it | Would a regulator or platform ask for it? |
| **Someone else demands it** | A client, platform or buyer requires it | Is a deal blocked until they have it? |
| **Insurance framing** | It prevents a loss they already fear | Would they buy it with the company's money, not their own? |
| **Performance pricing** | They pay out of money it created | Does the money arrive *before* it leaves? |

The last one is the strongest. **Performance pricing removes the question entirely** — if you're paid a share of what you recover, there is no leap of faith to make.

### The no-leap test (apply before building anything)

1. Would their accountant approve this line item?
2. Does a budget line for it already exist?
3. Can they compute the return in one napkin division?
4. Does someone else **require** it?
5. Does the money arrive before it leaves — or is payment contingent on the result?

**Yes to any two, and payment is not the risk.** None, and you're back to hoping.

### Eight ideas that pass

**1. Performance-priced recovery.** Rebuild failed or abandoned revenue for a niche and take a cut. E.g. recover failed subscription payments for small SaaS and take 20% of what you recover.
- *Justification:* they pay out of money that didn't exist without you.
- *Why it's the cleanest model in the file:* the buyer's downside is **zero**, so the objection is structurally impossible.

**2. Mandatory compliance for one narrow niche.** The document, certificate or filing a specific trade legally must have — privacy policies and cookie consent for small sites, industry permits, annual filings.
- *Justification:* legal necessity, and it **lapses**, so it's recurring by nature. Proven category (iubenda, Termly).
- *Why a niche wins:* generic tools exist; the money is in the specific, dreaded, jurisdiction-specific version.

**3. The displayable credential** — *the sweet spot.* A paid-for badge, score, certificate or verified metric, with criteria that actually mean something.
- *Justification:* it measurably wins work. *Bonus:* the payer displays it, so it markets itself (§11.5).

**4. Insurance-grade monitoring for a niche.** Watch the one thing that costs real money when it breaks — checkout, SSL, domain expiry, marketplace listing health, brand mentions.
- *Justification:* fear of a loss they already have. Recurring and cheap to run.

**5. The thing a client is blocking on.** Auto-answer the paperwork standing between a small vendor and a signed deal — vendor security questionnaires, client onboarding forms, tender documents.
- *Justification:* a deal is pending, so the payment is trivial against the deal size.

**6. Backup and export for a platform livelihood depends on.** "Back up and restore your Etsy / Shopify / Substack."
- *Justification:* insurance for an income. One-time plus recurring.

**7. No-show and revenue recovery for a local trade.** Booking, reminders and waitlist-filling for barbers, groomers, tattoo artists, physios.
- *Justification:* they can count the recovered revenue, and it's already a proven paid category (Booksy, Square Appointments).

**8. The annual filing chore for one country, one niche.** The thing freelancers dread and legally must do.
- *Justification:* obligation, dread, and recurrence — the three best motivators at once.

### What I'd actually pick, and why

- **#1 (performance pricing) is the only idea where "will they pay?" is not a question at all.** The fee comes out of money you created, so the buyer's downside is zero and the objection is structurally impossible. Its weakness is the mirror image: *you* only get paid if you perform, and you need a reachable pool of businesses with a measurable leak.
- **#3 (displayable credential) is the best balance** — measurable return for the payer, plus self-distribution, plus recurrence (criteria get re-issued), plus no audience needed on your side. It's why badges and awards lead the shortlist.
- **The combination nobody does well: credential + performance pricing.** Charge a small base, then take a cut of the work the credential wins. That removes the final objection — *"prove it works"* — and it is the strongest position in this entire file.

### The honest cost

Everything in this section is **competitive and boring.** Necessities have incumbents and no virality. The trade is real: **you are swapping a lottery ticket for a job.** That's the whole reason the ego ideas are seductive — they promise distribution without competition, but they only pay when an audience is already watching.

## 1. The mechanic: the Ego Engine

Every business in this file is a variation of one machine. Seven parts:

| # | Part | Why it's load-bearing |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A symbolic prize** — a title, a crown, a rank, a badge | Costs the operator ~$0 to grant. Infinite margin on the "reward". |
| 2 | **A public scoreboard** | The win must be *provable* and visible, or nobody cares who won. |
| 3 | **Payment converts directly into score** | Money → points. This is the entire monetization. No inventory, no fulfillment. |
| 4 | **A unit of identity the payer belongs to** | "My city", "my breed", "my school", "my band". People spend on *their affiliation*, not themselves. |
| 5 | **A named rival** | Abstract "be #1" is weak. "Beat Dallas" is a purchase trigger. |
| 6 | **A legitimacy layer** | Some plausible reason it's a real contest — a tally, an end date, a jury, a cause. Without it, it reads as a scam and payments die. |
| 7 | **A reset** — end date, decay, or season | Without a reset there is one winner and everyone else leaves forever. |

### Why it works psychologically

It is **not** buying a product. It's buying **identity confirmation**. Two consequences:

- **Payment feels like participation, not consumption.** You're not purchasing; you're *supporting your side*. That's why the same person who won't pay $5/month for an app will pay $50 to push their hometown up a list.
- **Social cover.** "I'm supporting my city / my shelter / my team" is a noble act. "I'm buying rank for myself" is vain. The same transaction, wrapped differently, converts completely differently. **Always choose the version that grants the payer moral cover.**

## 2. The most important variable: how many people share the identity

This is the single biggest predictor of whether one of these works. Payment pools; the bigger the pool, the higher the ceiling.

**The aggregation ladder (weak → strong):**

```
1 person      → personal vanity            weak: no pooling, no spread
family        → "our dog", "our kid"       modest
club / fandom → "our band", "our team"     strong: thousands share it
school/alumni → "our alma mater"           very strong: shared identity + money
breed / craft → "our breed", "our trade"   strong: tribes, endlessly competitive
city          → "our city"                 very strong: hundreds of thousands
country       → "our nation"               strongest reach, highest political risk
```

**Why group identity beats personal vanity:** a city of 300,000 people all want *the same entity* to win. So hundreds of small payments aggregate into one giant score, and each payer only risks $1. Personal vanity boards (buy your own rank) cap at whatever one person will spend alone.

**The corollary that makes crowd-boards spread for free:** the payer's motivation to share is *in-group loyalty*, not personal gain. They will post "help my town win" to their entire network unprompted. **Your customers become your unpaid sales force.** That is the distribution engine, and it's why this genre beats normal ad buying.

## 3. Evidence this already works (receipts)

Before generating more, here's proof the category monetizes.

### America's Favorite Pet — the closest published analogue to the city idea
- Run by **Colossal Management, LLC** (Phoenix, AZ).
- Mechanics: **one free vote per 24 hours**, then **$1.00 per vote**. Paid votes are branded **"Hope Votes"** and directed to PAWS.org.
- Contestants are *told to ask family and friends to pay to vote* — the contestant becomes the distribution channel.
- It is **not** a vanish-with-your-money scam — it's a registered, operating business. That matters: the model is legal and durable.
- **But** it is documented in r/Scams and has a BBB complaint file describing exactly the failure mode to avoid: *"the system heavily promotes paid votes, creating a sunk-cost fallacy for families."*
- **Lesson:** the money is real. The *reputation damage* comes from exploiting sunk cost, not from charging. The honest version of this is available and under-defended.

### K-pop fan voting — fully industrialized at scale
- **CHOEAEDOL**: ~**310,000 ratings at 4.7★** on Google Play. **FANNSTAR**: ~17,500 ratings. Also THEKKING, ALLCHART, Kooky.
- Fans vote *dozens of times daily per account*, and voting power is purchasable ("coins"). Entire award shows and music-chart pre-votes route through these apps.
- This is the strongest proof that **"pay to make your side win" is a mass-market consumer behavior**, not a novelty.

### The software layer is already commoditized
- **izivote.com** — "the only voting platform with built-in paid voting. Charge per vote."
- **choicely.com** — contest voting apps, "generate revenue with paid votes."
- **easypromosapp.com**, **OpaVote** — same space.
- **Implication: the software is not the moat.** Anyone can rent this infrastructure this week. The moat is *the contest* — the identity, the rivalry, and the audience. Build the contest, rent the pipes.

### Mainstream media already runs the city version
- **Parade + Steller: "America's Favorite Small Town"** (Nov 2025) — a national search for the best small town, run by a magazine. The premise is validated by mainstream publishing; they just monetize it with ads instead of votes.

### From our own thread
- **outbid.lol** — pay-to-rank leaderboard, launched Aug 19 2026, built in ~3 hours. Live board showed **$17,001** for #1 and roughly **$200K+ cumulative** in gross bid totals. Unverified but publicly displayed.
- One documented warning from outbid: **a one-time purchase spikes once and dies.** Its traffic went from ~1.09M visitors in 48 hours to ~6,453/day six weeks later. *Rent and reset beat one-time purchase.*

## 4. The idea log

Format: **the pitch** → who pays → why → money → spread → risk.

### A. Place identity — the "my city" engine

**A1. Most Beautiful City in the World** *(the user's seed idea)*
- **Pitch:** A live global ranking. $1 = +1 to your city's tally. Live map, live totals, seasonal crown.
- **Who pays:** Anyone with hometown pride. Expats are the sharpest — distance makes the identity louder.
- **Money:** price × votes. At $1/vote and a modest 50,000 votes in season, that's $50K with near-zero cost of goods.
- **Spread:** City subreddits, local Facebook groups, expat communities, local news ("our city is #14, vote!").
- **Risk:** "Most beautiful" is a taste claim, so paid voting looks cynical. Better framing: *make the contest explicitly a popularity tally*, not an aesthetic judgement.

**A2. Best Small Town in [Country]** — the Parade precedent, self-run. Small towns have intense local media that will cover a national placing for free.

**A3. Best Neighborhood in [Metro]** — narrower, cheaper, and the rivalry is visceral because neighbors actually know each other.

**A4. National Dish / Best Cuisine (country vs country)** — enormous aggregation, enormous sharing. Handle with care: nationalism is the most flammable identity in the ladder.

**A5. Ugliest Roundabout / Worst Traffic / Best Pothole** — *negative* contests. Funnier, share further, and the "win" is municipal shaming — which gives local media a reason to cover it. Low stakes means low legitimacy risk.

### B. Group identity that already has money

**B1. Best University** — alumni bid to push their alma mater up.
- **Why this is the strongest money in the file:** alumni already donate **billions annually** to their schools out of pure identity. You are not creating a new spending behavior; you're relocating an existing one to a leaderboard.
- **Risk:** universities may object to their name in a paid vote. Use "alumni fanbase" framing.

**B2. Best High School in [State]** — school pride is lifelong and geographically dense. **Do not let minors pay** (see §6).

**B3. Best Fanbase in [League]** — fans pay to rank their club's *supporters* above rivals. Fandom is the purest ego engine that exists.

**B4. Best Marching Band / Best Student Section** — real subcultures with real, permanent rivalries and zero existing rankings.

**B5. Best Military Unit / Branch Rivalry** — intense identity, strong sharing. Handle with extreme care politically.

### C. Breed and tribe identity

**C1. Cutest Dog Breed** — ~200+ breeds, each with a ferociously loyal owner army.
- **Why it's strong:** breed owners *already* argue about this constantly. You're not creating the argument, you're charging admission to it. And the pooling works: every Golden owner wants *all* Goldens to win.
- **Money:** $1 votes, 200 entities, permanent year-round contention.

**C2. Best Rescue Dog / Cutest Shelter Dog** — the honest version of America's Favorite Pet.
- **The upgrade:** make the prize an **actual adoption**. Shelters then have a real reason to promote it to their donor network, so **the beneficiary becomes a distribution channel** — and the money genuinely goes somewhere.
- **Risk:** if the charity angle is decorative, you inherit the AFB reputation. If it's real, it's your best asset.

**C3. Best Cat Breed / Best Horse Breed** — horse people have significant money and intense breed loyalty.

**C4. Best Chicken / Pigeon / Rabbit Breed** — small communities, but *ferociously* competitive, and almost no online contest serves them.

### D. Fandom identity — the fully validated lane

**D1. Best K-pop Group / Idol** — already industrialized (see §3). The open angle is a **bracket-style World Cup** that resets seasonally, rather than an app chasing daily votes.

**D2. Best Anime Character** — fandoms organize multi-week vote campaigns *for free*, unprompted. You'd be monetizing behavior that already exists. **One of the highest-certainty ideas in this file.**

**D3. Best Video Game Character / Best NPC** — same mechanics, older-skewing audience with money.

**D4. Best Fictional Ship** (the pairing, not the boat) — shipping wars are the most durable and self-sustaining rivalry on the internet. Infinite seasons.

**D5. Best Underrated Band** — fans fund their favorite. Bands themselves will promote it for free because it's free publicity.

### E. Craft and profession identity *(the full wedding-vendor plan is logged in §7)*

**E1. Best Tattoo Artist in [City]** — status-obsessed, Instagram-native, already competitive.

**E2. Best Barista / Best Barber / Best Bartender in [City]** — small, local, and these trades *love* a public ranking. Bars and shops will put the badge in their window.

**E3. Best Wedding Vendor in [City]** — approved plan, see §7.

**E4. Best Indie Game** — developers will campaign their own audiences hard for a ranking.

### F. Cause-wrapped identity — the strongest legitimacy layer

*(Money + moral cover + the beneficiary promotes it for you.)*

**F1. Best Rescue Shelter in [State]** — shelters rally their entire donor base, which is exactly the network you want.
**F2. Most Deserving Teacher** — PTAs and parent groups fund it. **No payment from minors.**
**F3. Best Local Charity** — nonprofits bid their supporters in.
**F4. Best Firehouse / Best Ambulance Crew** — beloved locally, strong local-press pickup.
**F5. Best Service Dog Program** — high emotional stakes, real cause.

### G. Absurd and comedy contests — cheapest to run, fastest to spread

**G1. Worst Haircut** · **G2. Pettiest Office Grievance** · **G3. Most Cursed Photo** · **G4. Worst Parked Car in [City]** · **G5. Most Beautiful Pothole**
- **Why this section matters:** near-zero legitimacy risk, enormous shareability, and the tone is honest about being silly — so paid voting doesn't feel like a con. **Best low-budget first experiment in the file.**

### H. Meta / self-aware

**H1. Best Pay-to-Rank Site** — a board ranking the boards. Free press from every competitor you list.
**H2. Vote On Which Idea I Build Next** — publish this file, let people pay $1 to vote. The log *becomes* the launch, and it tests demand before any code exists.

### I. Arena structure variants (how the clock runs)

**I1. Daily Crown** — resets every 24h. Maximum repeat payment, minimum sunk cost.
**I2. Monthly Trophy + Hall of Fame** — past champions permanently listed. The hall of fame is the retention mechanic: it makes winning *permanent* enough to be worth paying for.
**I3. Dynasty Board** — a never-resetting all-time board alongside the seasonal one. Two boards, two motivations.
**I4. Decay / Rent** — rank lapses unless renewed monthly. This is the fix for outbid.lol's one-time-purchase weakness. **Rent beats sale.**

### J. Structural upgrades worth stealing

**J1. The reciprocity hook** — give a portion to a cause *the payer picks*. Converts a vanity payment into a donation with a leaderboard attached.
**J2. Proxy bidding** — let people bid on behalf of an entity they don't belong to ("adopt a town"). Opens the fan-base to everyone, not just locals.
**J3. The physical upsell** — the winner wants a *thing*. Sell a framed certificate, a trophy, a physical plaque, a winner's merch pack. This is honest, high-margin, and it's where the pet contests make real money.
**J4. The badge embed** — hand winners an embeddable badge/live widget for their website. Every embed is a permanent backlink and a free ad.
**J5. Free daily vote** — mandatory. It creates a habit, gives non-payers a stake, and keeps the board alive between paid bursts.
**J6. Public bid history** — show who paid what, when. outbid.lol's whole draw was the *spectacle* of the bidding. The transparency is the content.

### K. Races between people — individual competition

*(A different engine from the group-identity models above. Here the revenue comes from the competition itself, not from a shared identity. Full theory in [§9](#9-races-between-people-the-wider-theory).)*

**K1. "Most Beautiful Photo of Your Day" — daily photo contest** *(the user's seed idea)*
- **Pitch:** anyone posts one photo a day; the community votes; the daily winner takes a prize; the site earns from ads plus paid votes.
- **Why the race is the product:** every entrant must promote themselves to win, so **each entrant is a free distribution node**. And the entries themselves become the content that carries the advertising.
- **The honest problem with ads as the business:** display ad revenue is measured in single-digit dollars per *thousand* pageviews, and new/unbranded sites sit at the bottom of that range. To clear **$1,000/month on ads alone you'd need roughly 300,000+ pageviews a month.** That's a scale play, not a bootstrap play. *(RPM figures are approximate — worth verifying before betting on them.)* **Ads should be the third revenue layer, not the first.**
- **Fix the monetization order:** 1) paid extra votes / "keys", 2) a sponsor for the daily theme, 3) ads, 4) a physical winner upsell (print, certificate, trophy).
- **The single most valuable design change:** *don't* run "most beautiful photo". It's unbounded, unjudgeable, and the category is crowded and worthless. Run **a specific daily constraint** — *today's theme is your commute / one colour / a stranger's smile / your desk*. A constraint makes entries comparable, makes voting fast, makes the format repeatable, and turns it into a daily **habit** instead of a vague beauty contest.
- **Prize design:** award the prize for **winning**, never proportional to vote count. A cash prize that scales with votes directly rewards vote-buying and destroys trust. Fixed prize + a permanent Hall of Fame slot + something printable the winner can show off.
- **Two problems unique to 2026:** (a) **AI-generated images** — either verify originals or run a separate AI category, or the whole thing becomes a model-output showcase; (b) **copyright** — entrants must warrant in writing that they shot it.
- **Precedent:** **GuruShots** is this exact idea running at scale — see [§9.3](#93-the-mechanic-worth-stealing-from-gurushots-pay-with-time-or-money).

**K2. Head-to-head bracket battles**
- 1v1, single elimination, public. The bracket *is* the content and it manufactures appointment viewing ("we're losing, vote now!").
- Operator sells: paid votes, bracket sponsorship, ads.
- Best niches: fictional characters (anime, games, ships) — those fandoms already run these campaigns voluntarily, for free.

**K3. Speed races with a third-party buyer**
- Coding challenges, timed puzzles, buildathons.
- **The underused revenue:** *recruiters pay to see the leaderboard.* Companies already spend enormous sums on vetted engineering pipelines; a public timed leaderboard is a live, ranked, opt-in candidate pool. This is the clearest example of the third revenue surface in §9.2.
- Money: entry fees + recruiter access + sponsored categories.

**K4. Submission-fee awards (jury-judged)**
- The most proven model in this entire file, running for roughly a century: **the film festival**.
- Numbers: Sundance charges **$80–$125 per feature** and **~$55 per short**; a widely cited analysis put its submission revenue at roughly **$1,000,000**, while it accepts **under 1%** of entries. The prize is a laurel and the phrase "official selection".
- **Cost of the prize: effectively $0.** This is "winning is the reward, money for me" in its purest, most legal, most boring form.
- Why it scales: thousands of applicants × $50–$150, and **the rejection rate is what proves the award means something.**
- Niche variants: design awards, wedding-photography awards, indie game awards, coffee-roaster awards, tattoo awards, student film awards. Any trade with pride and $100 to spare.

**K5. Consistency / streak races**
- 30-day challenges, "who keeps going longest". The drama is attrition plus a daily reset.
- Money: subscription — the one model in this file where a subscription fits naturally — plus a sponsor.

**K6. Charity races (fundraising competitions)**
- Walk-a-thons, dance-a-thons, "which team raises most". Participants raise money from their own networks.
- Ancient, proven, and socially bulletproof. The operator takes a platform fee, or sells the tooling.

**K7. Prediction races — caution**
- Fantasy leagues and prediction contests. The money is real and large, but this is the closest model in this file to **regulated gambling**, and it sits on payment processors' restricted lists. **Do not start here.**

**K8. Escalation / dare races**
- Who does the most ridiculous thing, with public voting. Viral by construction, and the humour removes the legitimacy burden.

**K9. Curation-as-contest**
- "Best [thing] of the week" where the **entrant is a business** and the benefit is a badge they display. Bridges directly into the vendor models in §E/§F.

### L. Vanity tokens and personal-name boards

**L1. "Your name on the #1 slot" — personal name bid board** *(the user's idea)*
- **Pitch:** a page showing one name at the top. Anyone can take the #1 slot by paying $1 more than the current holder. Same mechanic as outbid.lol, applied to personal names.
- **Verdict: this is the weakest version of the mechanic.** It keeps outbid.lol's worst cost — you must buy your own traffic — while dropping the two things that made it work. It fails five of our own tests:

| Test | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **Aggregation (§2)** | It sits on the *bottom rung* of the ladder. One person = one payer. Nothing pools. A city board pools 300,000 people into one score; this pools nobody. |
| **A named rival (§1, part 5)** | A bid war requires two motivated competitors. `winning.com` reached $3,129 because businesses compete for attention. **Who is the rival for *your* name?** Nobody. Without an adversary, the $1-increment mechanic never escalates — it settles near the entry price. |
| **Spectators** | **The fatal one.** Status is only worth paying for if somebody witnesses it. outbid.lol worked because ~1M people came to watch; #1 meant something *because an audience saw it*. A name board with no traffic means being #1 is worth nothing. **You cannot sell status that nobody observes.** |
| **Display (the §11.2 pattern)** | Every surviving model works because the buyer **displays** the purchase — a badge on their own site, a check in their bio. A name on an unknown page isn't embeddable anywhere that matters, so **distribution is not included** and you must buy traffic yourself. |
| **Decay / recurring revenue (§5)** | It's a one-time purchase with no reset — outbid.lol's exact failure mode: 1.09M visitors in 48h down to ~6,453/day in six weeks (§7.1). |
- **Budget reality:** a $1–$10 buyer. To reach $1,000 you need 100+ distinct paid bidders at $10, versus **one** wedding vendor at $99. Plus the mechanic caps escalation at +$1 per move, so two rivals can ping-pong for pocket change.
- **Evidence from the clone board (§10):** the closest analogues — pay to own an identity token — are already listed there and they are *cheap*: `oneword.global` (pay to change one global word) at **$365**, `Title Atlas` (pay to own a country) at **$35–50**. The lane exists and has not produced a bid war.
- **Operational landmine:** a board where anyone can pay to display **any** name is an abuse vector — impersonation, harassment, defamation — and names are personal data under GDPR. You'd be moderating constantly, at a real cost, on near-zero revenue.

**L2. The salvage: surname, family, or clan boards**
- The mechanic is fine; **the entity is wrong.** Move the unit of identity up the ladder from *one person* to a *lineage*: **"the Smiths are #1."**
- That single change restores everything L1 was missing: **aggregation** (a surname can be shared by millions), a **named rival** (Smiths vs Joneses is an actual, ancient, self-sustaining contest), **shareability** (family pride and genealogy interest are enormous and under-served), and a **Hall of Fame** that accumulates.
- It becomes a *group-identity contest* (like the breed and city models in §C and §A), not a vanity board. Money: $1 votes, so it stays a scale play — see §5.

**L3. Reality check on name and handle spend**
There *is* real money in names, but it's mostly **utility** spend, not vanity: domain auctions have cleared **>$2,000,000 in a single 3-hour sale**, and a perfect-match domain can be worth millions to a company. The crucial distinction: **a domain is an address — it has function.** A name on somebody else's leaderboard is a *mention*, with no function. Domain economics do not transfer to mention boards, and this is the trap L1 walks into.

### M. "#1" concepts built for virality

*The user's position is right and our own receipts support it: people **do** pay for #1 — **$17,001** for outbid.lol's top slot, **$3,129** on its clone board, **$1,000/month** for an X Premium Business verification, **$50–$1,200/month** for a placement badge. The question was never* whether *people pay for #1. It's which **shape** of #1 goes viral *and* pays repeatedly.*

**The virality checklist** — what outbid.lol had and the personal-name board didn't:

1. **A one-sentence rule.** "Pay more, rank higher."
2. **Two named sides — never a flat ladder.** Conflict spreads; a position doesn't.
3. **A number that climbs in public.** The $5 → $14,013 ascent *was* the story journalists wrote about.
4. **A reward the winner must display** — an auto-generated share card.
5. **Decay or a season**, so the war restarts instead of ending.
6. **A notification when you're beaten.** Re-engagement and drama in one move.
7. **Launch into an audience already angry about the rivalry.**
8. **Be honest about the gimmick.** The joke is the marketing.
9. **Spectator content** — live ticker, bid history, a "wall of the fallen".
10. **A hard reset plus a permanent Hall of Fame.**

The name board failed because it had **none** of #2, #4, #6 or #7. The concepts below are each built to supply the missing parts.

---

**M1. The Dethrone — outbid with consequences** *(fixes the "no rival" problem)*
- **Rule:** "Outbid anyone by $1. They get told."
- Every bid **names its victim**. The displaced holder is notified — *"X just took your spot"* — which is simultaneously a re-engagement hook and a public callout.
- **Why it's viral:** each outbid generates *two* posts — the winner's brag and the loser's response. The map of grudges becomes the content.
- **Recurring:** rank expiry restarts the war every month.
- **Why it beats the name board:** it *manufactures* the rival that the name board lacked. The name board had nobody to beat; this one makes every participant somebody's target.

**M2. Turf war — teams, tiles, rent** *(fixes the pooling problem — likely the strongest candidate)*
- **Rule:** "Buy tiles for your team. Tiles decay every hour."
- **Why this is the best of the six:** it solves **aggregation** (a faction pools thousands of $1 payments into one score, which is exactly what individual vanity cannot do), it is *inherently* a duel (faction vs faction, so #2 comes free), the **map is the shareable artifact**, and **decay makes it rent** rather than a one-time purchase.
- Spectators watch the map move in real time — the same spectacle that pulled ~1M visitors to outbid.
- **Precedent:** r/place-style collaborative territory battles reached mass virality *without charging a cent*. Adding payment monetizes behaviour that has already been proven to spread.

**M3. A board for every real rivalry** *(fixes the distribution problem)*
- Not one board — **one board per actual argument**: iOS vs Android, tea vs coffee, Messi vs Ronaldo, tabs vs spaces.
- Each board is a self-contained viral node with **two pre-existing armies** that will share it for you.
- This is the fix for `whos1.com` ($205): its problem was never the mechanic, it was having **exactly one board and no distribution**. Hundreds of boards, each with two fanbases, is a different business.
- Keep it to opinions and generic categories, not trademarks.

**M4. Absurdly specific #1 boards**
- **Rule:** "Pay $1 to be #1 at something very specific."
- *"#1 at never having watched Star Wars." "#1 worst parallel parker." "#1 person most likely to text back."*
- **Why viral:** **the sharing motive is amusement, not prestige.** People share self-deprecating jokes far more readily than boasts — and it sidesteps the dilution problem entirely, because the board never claimed to matter.
- This is the honest inversion of the vanity board: **same mechanic, correct emotional trigger.**

**M5. Pay to say it — the one-sentence billboard**
- **Rule:** "$1 more and the sentence is yours."
- The top slot displays a sentence the holder writes (moderated). The content changes constantly, so there's a reason to return and read it — and screenshots of the current sentence are the ad.
- **Constrain the message** or it dies: it must be a compliment, a confession, or an answer to the day's debate. A bare "any text" board is where `oneword.global` ($365) sits — **proven weak**.
- **Cost:** moderation, and every message is an abuse surface.

**M6. Closed auction with a countdown and a Hall of Fame** *(fixes the staleness problem)*
- **Rule:** "The auction for #1 closes at midnight UTC."
- A visible countdown turns the final minutes into **sniping drama**, which is clippable and shareable. A fresh winner daily produces a permanent Hall of Fame, and the reset stops the board going stale — which is the exact failure mode of the current clone board, where every entry is stamped "last month" (§10).

**The honest caveat:** none of this makes virality controllable. These ten levers improve the odds; they don't guarantee the outcome, and the single biggest factor remains **launching into an audience that already cares about the rivalry.**

### N. Paid rivalry voting: the iOS vs Android case

**N1. The concept**
- Two sides, permanent, and **every smartphone owner is already on one of them**. Aggregation is maximal — this is the strongest identity pool in the entire file.
- Charge per vote. The product is **not a poll — it's a costly signal**: *"put your money where your mouth is."*
- **Why paid beats free here:** iOS-vs-Android polls are free everywhere, which is exactly why they're meaningless. A free click costs nothing and proves nothing. **Paying is the point** — and commitment is shareable ("I paid for Android, what did you do?").

**N2. The $0.50 question — fee math decides it, and the answer is market-dependent**
- The barrier is not the price; it's **entering card details**. And a **fixed per-transaction fee** can eat a small charge alive.
- **US-style card pricing (Stripe, roughly 2.9% + $0.30):** a $0.50 vote nets **~$0.18**. **63% gone to fees — $0.50 votes are uneconomic there.**
- **India (Razorpay ~2%; Cashfree ~1.75%; Stripe India ~2% + ₹0.80):** the fixed fee is ~₹0.80 ≈ $0.01. A ₹45 (~$0.50) vote nets ~₹44. **$0.50 votes work in India and fail in the US.**
- **UPI** may be cheaper still (possible zero merchant MDR — worth verifying with the processor), and one-tap UPI removes the card-entry friction that is the real conversion killer.
- **The counterweight nobody notices:** the market with the friendliest micro-payments is **India, which is ~95% Android** — and a 95/5 war is not a war. The most balanced split (the US, roughly even) has the fees that destroy $0.50 votes. *Fee-friendly market = lopsided rivalry; balanced rivalry = hostile fees.*
- **The resolution — sell bundles.** "10 votes for $5" keeps the cheap-per-vote psychology while batching the fixed fee: on $5, US fees take ~$0.44 (about **9%**, versus 63% on $0.50). Bundles make micro-votes viable in any market **and** raise average order value.
- **Benchmarks from the research:** America's Favorite Pet charged **$1.00 per vote**; GuruShots' entry actions were **$0.99 and $2.99**. Nothing found sells single $0.50 votes on card rails. *(Fee figures come from comparison articles, not primary processor docs — verify your own rates before pricing.)*

**N3. The verification idea — QR scanning does not do what you want**
- **A QR code is just a URL.** Anyone on any device can scan it, or simply open the link. **There is no device check in the scan itself.**
- **User-agent sniffing is trivially spoofable** — a desktop browser, a modified client, or a script can claim to be iOS.
- **The real tool exists, but it isn't a QR — it's OS-level attestation:**
  - **Apple App Attest** and **Google Play Integrity API** cryptographically verify that a request comes from a genuine, unmodified app installed from the official store. Play Integrity's own documentation: it verifies that "user actions and server requests originate from a genuine app installed by Google Play."
  - Cross-platform wrappers exist (Expo's `AppIntegrity`, Capacitor plugins) — so this is a **native-app path, not a web one**.
  - Even then it is not absolute: jailbroken, hooked, rooted, and emulated environments are the documented weak spots.
- **The cheaper and better answer: a paid vote is its own anti-cheat.** Cheating in a free poll is free; cheating here **costs money**. A bot farm buying votes is a *customer*. So don't over-engineer identity verification — **rate-limit spend per payment method and device fingerprint**, and publish raw totals. What you must protect is **fairness perception**, not absolute purity.
- **What the QR is genuinely good for: theatre.** A scan-to-vote flow makes the vote feel gated and official, which raises perceived stakes and therefore conversion. Use it for that — just don't mistake it for a security control, and never let money depend on it.

**N4. Two upgrades that beat a plain poll**
- **The loser's pot funds the winner's chosen charity.** Makes the loss sting, gives a defensible answer to "where does the money go?", and generates press ("Android fans just funded an iPhone charity"). It also inoculates against the America's Favorite Pet problem (§3) — there's a **real destination** for the money.
- **Many rivalries, not one.** A single board is the `whos1.com` failure (\$205, §10). Hundreds of duels, each with two pre-existing armies, is a different business (§M3).

### O. Designed for Instagram: concepts with a built-in reason to post

*§M covered the virality checklist for **boards**. This section is about the **object and the content** — what actually gets posted, and what makes a stranger do the posting for you.*

**Four rules — the mechanics, not luck**

1. **Design the artifact, not the page.** What spreads is never the website; it's the thing the website produces. **Decide the screenshot first**, then build the machine that makes it.
2. **Design the invitation, not the signup.** The strongest format makes *paying* an act that names another person — a challenge, a dethroning, a nomination. Every payment then generates a notification to somebody with a reason to respond. **Payment becomes distribution.**
3. **Design an episode, not a launch.** A format that produces a new post every day survives; a one-off spikes and dies. The board needs a *season*; the content needs an *episode*.
4. **Make the poster the hero, not the product.** The video is about *them* winning, losing or being absurd. If your logo needs explaining, it won't spread.

---

**O1. The Title Registry** — *the strongest fit*
- Real-world titles, publicly auctioned, with a **physical artifact** mailed to the holder: a framed certificate, a trophy, a plaque.
- Titles are absurd but genuinely wanted: *World's #1 Dad* (auctioned the week before Father's Day), *#1 Smith Family*, *Official #1 at [niche thing]*.
- **Three mechanics stack:**
  - **Gift purchases** — the buyer is *not* the subject (a child buys it for a parent, a squad buys it for their coach). **Gifting raises willingness to pay and hands distribution to the recipient**, who now has a reason to show everyone.
  - **The champion defends** — the holder pays monthly to keep the title. **That's the recurring revenue**, and it's driven by not wanting to lose a physical object everyone has already seen.
  - **Challenges name a rival** — anyone can publicly challenge a specific holder for a fee.
- **Why it's built for Instagram:** unboxing a physical trophy is the most shareable format on the platform, and every title defence is a fresh episode.
- **Trade-off:** this breaks the earlier "no customer contact" rule — you need a shipping address. It also adds real COGS (print + post), which is precisely *why* the price can be high.

**O2. The Challenge Chain** — payment *as* the invitation
- You pay to challenge a **named** person. The challenge is public and the challenged is notified: *"@X just paid ₹100 to challenge you at [thing]."*
- Every payment creates a new participant with a public reason to respond. **Growth is enforced by the mechanic** rather than hoped for.
- **Why it works:** the invite *is* the transaction, so distribution is not a separate job you have to do later.

**O3. The event-timed national board**
- The same paid backing board, but bound to a **real event** with a live audience — a World Cup, a final, an election. The event supplies urgency and a news cycle that carries you.
- **Honest warning:** `whos1.com`, the permanent and event-less version, sits at **$205** (§10). **The event is the entire difference** — it creates a deadline and a crowd. Launch windows recur every two years.

**O4. The live wall during a match**
- Fans pay to put club colours or a short message on a screen that updates during the game. Time-boxed to 90 minutes, so urgency is total — and the screen is the screenshot.

**O5. The absurdist leaderboard**
- Not prestige — comedy: *#1 at never having watched Star Wars.* The sharing motive is **amusement**, a far easier share than a boast (§M4), and it removes the dilution problem entirely because the board never claimed to matter.

**Honest costs and odds**
- Physical fulfilment is real work: addresses, printing, shipping, replacements.
- "Official" needs care — don't imply a real governing body, and avoid prize + payment + chance, which is lottery law (§6).
- **Virality remains uncontrollable.** These rules raise the odds; the deciding factor is whether the format can generate a genuinely interesting new episode every day — and whether you post fifty times before judging it.

## 5. Business model summary

**Revenue shapes, in order of how fast they start:**

1. **Pay-per-vote** — $1 (or $5/$10 tiers). Impulse-priced, unlimited volume, no fulfillment. The engine.
2. **Pay-for-placement / rent** — a vendor or entity pays monthly to hold a rank. Recurring. (This is the wedding-vendor model, §7.)
3. **Physical winner upsell** — certificates, trophies, merch. High margin, honest, and it's where the emotional buyer converts hardest.
4. **Sponsorship** — once a contest has attention, a city tourism board, a pet brand, or a media outlet pays to be attached to it.
5. **Ads** — only meaningful at K-pop-voting-app scale.
6. **The audience itself** — entrants and voters are a permissioned list of people who care intensely about a niche. That's the asset that survives any one contest.

**Cost structure:** the prize is $0, the inventory is $0, and the software can be rented (see §3). Real costs are payment fees, hosting, and *your time getting the first crowd to show up*.

**The honest arithmetic:** at $1/vote you need enormous volume. At $25–$100/placement you need 10–40 payers. **The placement/rent model reaches $1,000 far faster than the vote model** — the vote model is a scale play, the placement model is a bootstrap play.

## 6. What kills these (read before building)

**Reputation**
- The known failure mode is documented and specific: *America's Favorite Pet* has a BBB complaint file and an r/Scams thread **because it leans on sunk cost** — pushing paid votes onto families who've already lost. That, not the charging, is what destroyed its reputation.
- **The line:** charge for visibility or participation and say so plainly (outbid.lol's model: *"Rank is what you pay — nothing else"*). Never imply bought rank equals quality, and never manufacture despair to extract another purchase.

**Payment processing**
- Gambling, contests, and wagering sit on restricted-business lists. "Pay to vote" gets flagged and can get funds frozen mid-campaign.
- Mitigations: frame as **paid placement or support**, not chance; keep payouts/prizes modest; be ready to talk to a high-risk processor; never let the *operator* hold a pot.

**Regulatory (the real one)**
- **Prize + payment + chance = lottery** in many jurisdictions. That's the trap.
- Safer structures: it must be **skill- or effort-based**, or offer a genuine **free alternative method of entry** (the sweepstakes approach). If money is required to win, get a lawyer for your jurisdiction before launch, not after.

**Platform rules**
- Inside a phone app, **digital votes must go through Apple/Google IAP** — Stripe is not allowed. This is a common way these projects get rejected.
- Contest ads on Meta/Google have their own disclosure requirements.

**Protect the crowd from a whale**
- The central design tension: **pay-to-win kills the crowd.** If one rich entrant can buy an unbeatable lead, everyone else stops playing and your volume dies with them.
- Fixes: per-entity caps, free daily votes that matter, tiered brackets, or a jury component. Decide this *before* launch.

**Minors**
- School, youth-sport, and teacher contests must not accept payment from children. This is a legal and reputational landmine.

**Prizes**
- Cited directly in the research: **"low monetary value prizes will discourage entrants from paying for votes."** A symbolic crown works *only* if the public attention is genuinely high. If attention is low, the prize must be real.

## 8. Cheapest way to test any of these

The mistake is building software first. The order should be:

1. **Pick a contest with an existing argument** (anime characters, dog breeds, cities) — never one you have to convince people is interesting.
2. **Run season one with no software.** One landing page, a live tally, a $1 payment link, a hand-updated leaderboard. Cost: a domain and an afternoon.
3. **Post where the identity already lives** — the subreddit, the Facebook group, the city forum. Say plainly that votes are paid for.
4. **Watch one number:** do people share it *without being asked*? If yes, the aggregation is real. If no, the identity pool is too small — switch entities, keep everything else.
5. **Only then build**, and rent the voting infrastructure (§3) before writing a leaderboard by hand.
6. **Add the reset and the rent** before scaling — otherwise you've built outbid.lol, which printed money for one week and then stopped.

**Ranking the ideas by how fast a solo operator could reach the first $100:**
`G-section comedy contests` (no legitimacy burden) → `C1 dog breeds / D2 anime characters` (existing arguments, huge pools) → `A1 city tallies` (biggest pool, heaviest sharing) → `B1 alumni` (most money, slowest to organize) → `E/F` placement and cause models (highest revenue per payer, needs a supply of participants).

## 9. Races between people: the wider theory

### 9.1 Why a race is a better engine than a product

A race makes the competitors manufacture the operator's four inputs for free:

| The operator needs | The race provides it | Because |
|---|---|---|
| **Content** | The entries | Entrants produce it in order to compete |
| **Distribution** | The entrants' promotion | Winning requires advocates, so they recruit their own networks |
| **Engagement** | Spectators | Watching a live contest is entertainment |
| **Revenue** | Entry fees + paid votes | Competitors pay to compete; fans pay to help |

**Competition arbitrage:** you sell status and information to competitors, while the competitors build the product and bring the audience.

### 9.2 The three revenue surfaces

Most operators only ever bill one of them. There are three:

1. **From competitors** — entry fees, paid votes, premium profiles, extra submissions, "keys".
2. **From spectators** — ads, paid votes, early or exclusive results, subscriptions.
3. **From third parties** — sponsors, recruiters, local businesses, brands wanting a concentrated niche audience.

**Surface 3 is the most under-exploited and the most profitable**, because a third party pays for *access* rather than for a trinket. Whenever you design a race, ask immediately: **who would pay to be attached to the winner?** If the answer is nobody, the race is a hobby.

### 9.3 The mechanic worth stealing from GuruShots: pay with time *or* money

**GuruShots** is the photo-contest idea running at commercial scale (~48,650 ratings at 4.1★ on Google Play). Its monetization is more clever than a simple paywall:

- Entrants buy **keys** (~$2.99) to add an extra photo, and **fills** (~$0.99) to swap a weak photo out. Challenges must be unlocked.
- **You must vote on other people's photos to climb — or buy "fills" to skip the voting.** So the participant pays with **time** or with **money**, and the operator wins either way: free labour that runs the ranking, or cash.
- **The prizes are usually more keys and fills** — roughly $200–$300 of platform credit. **The prize therefore costs the operator almost nothing and recycles the winner straight back into the paid loop.** This is the purest expression of "winning is the reward, money for me" that I found anywhere in this research.
- **Its cautionary half:** winners skew heavily toward the paying "Gurus" — the pay-to-win problem from [§6](#6-what-kills-these-read-before-building) showing up in a real product — and users complain that voting is random and unconsidered. **Copy the monetization; fix the fairness.**

### 9.4 The competition taxonomy

| Race type | Who does the work | What you sell | Proven example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vote-based popularity | Entrants promote | Paid votes, ads, sponsor | America's Favorite Pet, GuruShots |
| Head-to-head bracket | Entrants promote | Paid votes, bracket sponsor | Character polls |
| Jury-judged award | Applicants submit | Submission fees | Film festivals (Sundance) |
| Speed / ranked | Competitors grind | Entry fees, **recruiter access** | Coding challenges |
| Streak / consistency | Participants persist | Subscription, sponsor | 30-day challenges |
| Fundraising | Participants fundraise | Platform fee | Walk-a-thons |
| Prediction | Entrants analyse | Pool rake | Fantasy sports — *regulated* |

### 9.5 The test: is a given race worth running?

A race is worth building only if **all** of these hold:

1. **Amateurs massively outnumber professionals.** The top 1% never pay for this; the long middle pays the bills.
2. **Self-promotion is required to win** — without it, entrants bring you nothing and you're just hosting a file upload form.
3. **The entries are presentable content** — they must carry the ads and feed SEO.
4. **The prize can be non-cash** — credit, laurels, badges, a Hall of Fame slot. Cash prizes eat the margin.
5. **The result is permanent and displayable** — the winner keeps using it (badge, laurel, certificate), which makes them a repeat distributor.
6. **There is a reset** — daily or seasonal, or the crowd leaves after one round.
7. **A third party would pay to be attached to the winners.** That's the profit.

### 9.6 Design rules

- **Constrain the entry.** "Most beautiful" is unbounded and unvotable. "Your commute, shot today" is comparable, fast to judge, and habit-forming.
- **Free entry, paid amplification.** Free entry maximises volume and content; charge for *votes and keys*, not for admission. Jury-judged awards are the exception — a submission fee only works once the award already carries prestige.
- **Never tie cash to vote count.** It rewards buying votes and kills trust. The prize goes to the winner, not to the loudest spender.
- **Give spectators one free vote a day.** It builds the habit and keeps the board alive between paid bursts.
- **Moderate for AI and theft.** In 2026 a photo or art contest must either verify an original (EXIF, capture date, or a constraint only a live photographer could satisfy) or run a separate AI category. Require a written authorship warranty.
- **Publish the vote and bid history.** Transparency is the content — outbid.lol's entire draw was watching the money move in public.

## 10. Competitive intel: the outbid.lol clone board (Sept 2026)

Reading outbid.lol's own **"Leaderboards & Attention Markets"** category board carefully — it's the single most useful competitive document we have.

**What the board shows**
- Top bid in the category: **$3,129** (`winning.com`). Then **$501**, $365, $205, $155, $154, $153, $151, $150, $120, $118, $100 — with a long tail at **$50, $35, $10**.
- Most entries are stamped **"last month"**, i.e. the amounts are static and **no bid wars are happening**.
- The daily board is empty: **"Nobody has claimed a rank since midnight UTC."**
- Compare to the parent board's early peak of **$17,001 for #1**. The clone economy is an order of magnitude thinner.

**Four readings**

1. **These figures are marketing spend, not revenue.** A listing here is a clone buying *our* attention — `winning.com` paid $3,129 to be #1 on a competitor's leaderboard. That only makes sense if your customer is *other builders*. **So the clone economy is aimed at itself — a circular audience, which is a bubble signature.**

2. **The generic mechanic is saturated and not monetizable as a category.** 60+ clones, all listed on the parent, all chasing the same builder audience. The parent captured the liquidity (the moat described in [§3](#3-evidence-this-already-works-receipts)); the clones split the leftovers. **Do not enter as "another pay-to-rank board."**

3. **Someone already built the country-pride idea.** `whos1.com` — *"Live world ranking of all 211 football nations. £1 = 1 POWER. Back a country. Take #1."* That is the user's "back your city / back your country" concept, already shipped — **sitting at $205.** So the pure crowd-tally board has been tested by someone else and is flat. **Important correction to our earlier framing: consumer pride alone does not produce a bid war.**
   - Also on the board: `oneword.global` ($365, pay to change one global word), `mostexpensive.love` ($153, paid love notes), `biggestlogowins.com` ($151, buy a share of the screen), `Title Atlas` (pay to own countries, $35–50), `channel01.tv` ($100, pay to be on air), `adultbid.lol`, `OnlyRank` (OnlyFans models), `outbid.immo` (real estate, $501), `outbid.az` (Azerbaijani brands), `thonest.com`, and `outsize.lol`.

4. **Vertical pay-to-rank for businesses has been tried, and is thin so far.** `outbid.immo` (real estate) sits at **$501**; `winning.com` ("for businesses") at **$3,129**. Notably, **no wedding, no local-service, and no profession-specific board appears on the list at all.** (Again: these are *their* marketing spend, not their revenue — weak traction in one channel isn't proof of a dead business, but it isn't encouraging either.)

**What this changes for us**

- **The mechanic is a commodity; the audience is the entire bet.** Confirmed by [§3](#3-evidence-this-already-works-receipts): anyone can clone this in a weekend. So 100% of the value must come from *who pays* and *why they can't get this elsewhere* — the wedding vendors' documented grievance against The Knot, plus their existing **$50–$1,200/month** placement habit.
- **Don't launch into the attention-market category.** No "pay-to-rank" framing on the homepage. The product is *"a public ranking for wedding photographers in Austin"* — not *"another bid board."*
- **The unproven question is now narrow and testable:** will a wedding vendor pay ~$99 for a badge plus visibility? **Nobody on that board has answered it, because nobody on that board sells to buyers with real marketing budgets.** That is either the opening or the trap, and [§8](#8-cheapest-way-to-test-any-of-these) makes it cheap to find out.
- **The distinction that actually matters:** thin bid-war boards fail (this entire list). Consumer contests work at scale via *game mechanics and volume* (GuruShots, [§9.3](#93-the-mechanic-worth-stealing-from-gurushots-pay-with-time-or-money); America's Favorite Pet, [§3](#3-evidence-this-already-works-receipts)). **A bid war only works when the payers have budget and a named rival** — which is the wedding-vendor case, and not the city-pride case.

## 11. Independent dig: how software actually converts ego into money

*(Deliberately separate from the contest mechanics above — this is the underlying lever set, the ways software charges for ego **without** running a contest at all.)*

### 11.1 The lever catalog

| Lever | What you're really selling | Receipt | Build difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pay to display a rank badge** | Permission to show proof | **G2**: a free profile may only show "Users Love Us"; **Leader / High Performer badges require a paid plan**, and since Summer 2025 most new badges are vendor-exclusive. Vendors then put the badge on **their own website**. | Low |
| **Pay to be verified** | Relief from being doubted | **X Premium**: $3–$40/mo tiers; **X Premium Business Full Access is $1,000/mo**. As of 2026 there is **no free path to a blue check**. | Low |
| **Pay to see your own validation** | Curiosity about your own desirability | Dating apps: unlocking "who liked you" is described as **the #1 revenue driver**. Tinder **deliberately hides the faces**. Bumble Premium ≈ $60/mo; Hinge $14.99–$29.99/mo. | Medium (needs two-sided users) |
| **Pay to hold a rank you're already winning** | Fear of losing standing | **Duolingo** leagues and streaks lifted retention **12% → 55%**; **Strava**'s single biggest retention driver is the **Segment** (KOM crowns). | Medium |
| **Pay to skip the labour** | Time, traded for standing | **GuruShots "fills"** (~$0.99) let you skip voting; **keys** (~$2.99) add entries. | Medium |
| **Pay to prove a claim** | Escape from suspicion | **thonest.com** (seen on the outbid board): your revenue pulled from your payment processor into an embeddable live timeline, "so prospects do not have to take your word for it." | Medium |
| **Pay to own an identity token** | A name that is yours | Vanity domains and ENS `.eth`; `oneword.global` ($365 for one global word); license plates; vanity phone numbers. | Low |
| **Pay to gift status** | Your own generosity, publicly | Twitch bits, TikTok gifts — the top-supporter leaderboard is the actual product. | Medium |
| **Pay to be exclusive** | Belonging | Invite-only rooms, tiered memberships, early access. | Low |
| **Pay to erase** | Dignity | Paying to hide your own activity or history from view. | Low |

### 11.2 Why ego pricing beats utility pricing

This is the commercially important part. Utility software anchors its price to value delivered, so the buyer comparison-shops and churns. Ego software doesn't:

- **No anchor.** There is no competing "cheaper badge". You cannot buy the same standing elsewhere, so price sensitivity collapses.
- **Non-transferable.** A rank, a checkmark, a badge cannot be resold — no secondary market, no arbitrage.
- **Revocable.** The crucial one: **status can be taken away, so it can be rented instead of sold.** A utility is bought once; standing must be *maintained* (G2 reissues badges quarterly). Revocability is what converts a one-time payment into a subscription — the same decay mechanic I recommended for the board, but applied to the *good* rather than to a timer.
- **Self-advertising.** Payers **display** what they bought — G2 vendors paste badges onto their own sites, X users announce verification, Strava users post KOMs. **Distribution comes free with the purchase.**

### 11.3 The two rules the research actually supports

**Rule 1 — Segment the competition, and give everyone a win.**
Global leaderboards *lower* engagement, because you're ranked against people vastly better than you. Duolingo's fix is small leagues of similarly-matched learners; Strava's fix is auto-generated **personal bests**, so a rider who will never take a KOM still gets a win to look at. Applied to us: a vendor will never be #1 in a large market — so **the loser needs something to display too**, or they stop playing.

**Rule 2 — Sell the reveal, not the product.**
The highest-yield ego moment in the whole dig is **information about yourself you cannot otherwise see** (who liked you). It costs the operator nothing to produce and has no substitute.

### 11.4 The central paradox: selling status destroys the status

This is the most important structural finding of the dig, and it applies directly to the approved plan.

**Status is a non-renewable resource that your own revenue model depletes.** The moment a blue check became purchasable by anyone, it stopped signalling anything — which is exactly what happened to it. Sell status and you dilute the asset you're selling.

There are only two honest escapes, and you must pick one:

1. **Be blatantly explicit that it's paid.** Charge for *attention*, own the joke, never imply quality. This is outbid.lol's model: *"Rank is what you pay — nothing else."* It survives dilution because it never claimed to mean anything.
2. **Keep the rank genuinely earned and charge for the display.** This is **G2's model**, and it's the stronger business: badges are paid for but *granted on real verified reviews*, so the badge keeps its meaning while revenue scales. **You charge for the right to show it, not for the position itself.**

Option 2 is meaningfully better. It converts an ethical problem into a moat — because the earned criteria are the hard part to copy, and the badge stays worth displaying.

### 11.5 The tiered-badge unlock (why G2 out-earns a single #1 slot)

The insight that generalises furthest: **G2 doesn't sell one top spot — it sells a family of tiers** (Leader, High Performer, Momentum Leader, Best ROI…), reissued quarterly, per category. So instead of **one** vendor paying for #1, **dozens** pay for *something* to display.

Applied to our plan: selling "#1 in Austin" caps you at one payer per board. Selling **"Top 10 in Austin · 2026"** — a badge any vendor in the top 10 (or top quartile, or "best reviewed", "most responsive", "best value") can buy and display — converts **the losers into the revenue.** Combined with quarterly reissue, that's a subscription paid by the 90% who don't win.

**This is the most valuable change to come out of this dig, and it should supersede the flat "one #1 slot" design.**

### 11.6 Where the line is

The same research that shows the money also shows the failure modes:

- **Hostage mechanics.** Deliberately concealing matches to force payment is the *documented* #1 revenue driver in dating — and also the most criticised thing in that category, drawing regulatory attention. **Withholding something the user has a moral claim to is the line.**
- **Fake scarcity and sunk cost** — the America's Favorite Pet pattern (§3).
- **Fairness collapse.** GuruShots winners skew to payers; "are G2 rankings paid for?" is a live criticism. If paying visibly buys the rank, the crowd leaves ([§6](#6-what-kills-these-read-before-building)).
- **Dilution** — see 11.4. Every sale cheapens the asset.

### 11.7 What I'd build from this dig

Ranking the levers by fit with our actual constraints *(solo, no audience, self-serve payments, no customer contact, weeks not months)*:

1. **Tiered displayable badges for a trade that already pays for placement** — highest fit. The buyer has budget, the buyer displays the badge (free distribution), badges reissue quarterly (recurring revenue), and the rank can stay genuinely earned (the G2 model). **This is the wedding plan, upgraded.**
2. **Pay-to-prove** — verified metrics you embed on your own site. Cheap to build, business buyers, but depends on a data-source integration.
3. **Pay-to-see-your-validation** — the highest revenue density in consumer software by a wide margin, but it needs a two-sided user base we can't conjure without an audience.
4. **Segmented leagues** — only works bolted onto an activity people already do daily. We don't have one.

**Conclusion of the dig:** the money in ego is not in the contest. It's in **selling permission to display proof of standing — in tiers, on a renewable clock, to people who already spend money to look credible.** The contest was one way to manufacture the standing. **The badge is the product.**

## 12. Open questions

- Which of these can hold a **seasonal reset** without losing the crowd's emotional investment?
- Where is the line between a cap that protects the crowd and a cap that kills the whales' willingness to pay?
- Can a **free alternative method of entry** keep a paid vote contest out of lottery regulation while still converting? (Worth researching properly before committing money.)
- Which entities have *existing, uncaptured* argument energy — i.e. people already fighting about this with no scoreboard to fight on? That's the highest-signal place to look for the next idea.
- **Will a wedding vendor pay ~$99 for a rank badge plus visibility?** This is now the single blocking question for the approved plan — nobody on the clone board has tested a payer with a real marketing budget.
- **Will brides actually visit the board?** The traffic side is the weakest link: a badge with no couple-side audience is a vanity purchase that churns in month two.
- Is there a vertical where the payer has **budget**, the market has a **named rivalry**, *and* the consumer side (the actual buyers) is cheap to reach? That combination is the real target — wedding vendors satisfy the first two and are uncertain on the third.
- **Should the wedding plan be rebuilt around tiered badges instead of one #1 slot?** ([§11.5](#115-the-tiered-badge-unlock-why-g2-out-earns-a-single-1-slot)) Doing so converts the ~90% of vendors who never win into payers, but it requires the rank to be *earned* by criteria rather than bought — a materially different product from the one approved.
- Can we hold the **earned-rank / paid-display** split without the review volume that gives G2 its credibility? With no existing user base, where do the "verified" criteria come from on day one?
- Is there any identity unit **between one person and a city** that pools enough to fund a bid war — a surname, a school, a trade, a diaspora? (§4 L2 bets on surnames; untested.)
